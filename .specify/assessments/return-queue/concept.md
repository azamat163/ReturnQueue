# Concept: Return Queue

- **Slug**: return-queue
- **Created**: 2026-10-03
- **Recommended option**: A — limited validation experiment.

## Options

### Option A — Validate the Complete Journey

- **Sketch**: test a simple interactive journey: add items with user-confirmed deadlines,
  group them by drop-off place, retain submission receipts, and mark refunds received.
  Compare it with the participant's existing workflow on their own recent purchases.
- **Appetite**: small; proposed ceiling of one working week for preparation and synthesis,
  excluding time waiting for recruitment. This is a budget choice, not a delivery estimate.
- **Trade-offs**: fast learning about adoption; no automatic import or verified store catalog.
- **Rabbit holes**: route optimization, retailer integrations, OCR perfection, automatic
  bank reconciliation, and extending into warranties/subscriptions.

### Option B — Broad Automated Purchase Assistant

- **Sketch**: import purchases automatically and coordinate policies, deadlines and refunds.
- **Appetite**: large; months, unjustified with current evidence.
- **Trade-offs**: potentially less entry effort, but much more operating work and sensitive
  data handling; direct overlap with established advertised competitors.
- **Rabbit holes**: email access, parsers, exceptional policies, identity and backend support.

### Option C — Use Existing Tools

- **Sketch**: test Ajar or Fyld alongside store order pages and reminders; document the
  workflow. If adequate, stop this candidate and investigate another problem.
- **Appetite**: small; proposed two-day hands-on audit budget.
- **Trade-offs**: lowest build cost; does not create a differentiated product for the founder.
- **Rabbit holes**: mistaking feature-list differences for a customer advantage.

## Recommendation

Combine A's limited experiment with C's comparison. Do not invest in a complete app until
the differentiating benefit and capture habit are observed. No current evidence supports B.

## Out of Scope (for the recommended option)

- Email/bank access, backend services, AI summaries, maps, warranty management.
- Automatic claim submission or automatic promises about store policies.
- A public launch, outreach, charges, or paid advertising without user authorization.

## Assumptions to Validate

- Grouping drop-offs saves meaningful coordination effort.
- People will capture and update items without email access.
- The refund follow-through creates value beyond existing tools.
- A usable business can emerge at a low consumer price.

Pricing experiment: compare a $19.99 one-time local utility with a $14.99/year offering.
These are proposed offers, not approved prices. Test recurring willingness to pay and
recurring value before choosing a subscription. Model costs before promising lifetime access.
